In substance abuse treatment, we often meet clients who specifically choose **flower** (also called bud, herb, or weed) over concentrates or hash. For outsiders, it might all seem like the same substance, but the reality is significantly different — especially regarding the experience, potency, and patterns of habituation.

Perhaps the biggest advantage of flower, as highlighted by many clients, is the ability to smoke it without mixing it with tobacco. This eliminates the nicotine addiction, which often keeps clients trapped in substance abuse long after they actually want to quit cannabis.

That said, flower today is not the same low-potency product as in the 70s. Modern cultivation techniques have created a broad spectrum of extremely specialized and potent plants.

Here is what you, as a counselor, should know.

## The Difference Between Concentrates and Flower

While hash and other concentrates are extracted resin (trichomes) from the plant, flower is simply the dried and cured female buds.

Because the plant material is still intact, flower burns differently and retains a much more complex profile of **terpenes** — the aromatic oils that give cannabis its smell (e.g., pine needle, citrus, earth, or berry).

It is a widespread misconception that flower is automatically weaker than concentrates. A clean, high-quality concentrate will almost always have a higher percentage of THC per gram than flower itself, because concentrates are *extracted* material.

Nevertheless, the client may experience the high from flower as more "intense." This is due to several factors:
1. Flower is often smoked without tobacco, which means a whole joint can contain more cannabis overall if the client isn't paying attention to dosage.
2. The intact terpene profile in the flower creates a so-called "entourage effect," which can amplify the psychotropic effects of the THC.

## Indica vs. Sativa (And Why It's Almost a Myth Today)

When clients talk about what type of flower they smoke, they almost always refer to two overarching categories: **Indica** and **Sativa**.

*   **Sativa-dominant strains:** Known for providing a "head high" — a more energetic, uplifting, creative, and stimulating experience. Clients often use it during the day.
*   **Indica-dominant strains:** Known for providing a "body stone" — a heavy, relaxing, sedative, and pain-relieving experience. ("Indica = In da couch"). Clients often use it in the evening to sleep.

**Clinical insight:** Modern science shows, however, that the botanical classification tells us very little about the high. Almost all modern cannabis is hybrids (crossbreeds). The crucial factor is not whether the plant is broad- or narrow-leaved (Indica/Sativa), but rather its **chemical profile** — specifically the terpene composition.

Terpenes like *Myrcene* (found in hops/mangoes) have a strongly sedative effect and are often found in "indica" strains, while *Limonene* (found in citrus) is uplifting and often dominates "sativa" strains.

## Understand Your Client's Choice

In the modern cannabis market, practically all flower is named after its exact lineage ("strain"), e.g., *Sour Diesel*, *Granddaddy Purple*, or *Gelato*. This is far more widespread than with concentrates or hash.

If you want to understand *why* a client smokes the exact type they do, it can be enormously rewarding to look up their preferred strain. Websites like [Leafly.com](https://www.leafly.com/) function as a complete encyclopedia of cannabis strains.

When the client tells you they smoke *Blue Dream*, you can look it up and see: "Ah, Leafly says it primarily helps with stress and depression, but can cause dry mouth and anxiety in large quantities." This provides an entirely new starting point for the therapeutic conversation about self-medication.
